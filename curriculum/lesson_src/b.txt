4.1|018|A gas network is nodes (basins, hubs, markets) joined by edges (pipelines) with capacities.|Supply-push corridors exist because a basin must clear; demand-pull corridors because a market must be fed.|Flows can reverse when supply patterns change, as they did when shale shifted production.|Hubs sit where many pipelines meet and gas can be rerouted.|A regional map is the first deliverable in any basis analysis.
4.2|016|A tariff lists the services, rates and rules a pipeline must offer every shipper.|Most fixed costs are recovered through the reservation charge.|A contract names primary receipt and delivery points where the shipper has the highest priority.|Shippers may split a path into segments or use other points at lower priority.|A rate sheet gives reservation, usage and fuel for each service and zone.
4.3|016|Primary firm service is scheduled first and cut last.|Interruptible service is cheap per unit but flows only when capacity is spare.|Secondary firm ranks between primary firm and interruptible.|Under constraint, the pipeline cuts interruptible, then secondary, then primary pro rata.|Firm wins at high load factors; interruptible wins when capacity is rarely needed.
4.4|014|Operationally available capacity is what a pipeline posts as unscheduled at a point.|Repeated scheduling cuts at a point signal a constraint.|Planned maintenance is posted in advance and lowers capacity for a known period.|When a constraint tightens, basis on either side widens fast.|Capacity postings let you see congestion before prices fully react.
4.5|014|A firm shipper can release unused capacity to another shipper for a period.|In an asset management agreement a marketer runs a holder's capacity and shares the value.|Releases are either posted for bidding or prearranged with a named replacement shipper.|Released capacity is worth about the expected spread it can capture.|Secondary capacity lets you get transport without a long-term contract.
4.6|011|Western Canada's basin, led by the Montney, supplies Canada and exports to the US.|Large gathering and mainline systems move that gas to Alberta markets and export points.|AECO is the Alberta benchmark and usually trades below Henry Hub.|Exports cross into the US Northwest, Midwest and Northeast.|Canadian gas flows south when the price difference covers transport.
4.7|035|US pipeline exports to Mexico are a major, steady demand source.|Mexican demand is driven by gas-fired power and industry and depends on in-country pipelines.|Much of the export gas comes from the Permian, so Mexican takeaway affects Waha prices.|Border flow data from each side must be reconciled before use.|Cross-border flows carry regulatory, payment and infrastructure-delay risks.
4.8|032|Liquefaction chills gas to a liquid for shipping; capacity is counted per train.|Shipping cost depends on distance, charter rates and boil-off.|Regasification terminals return LNG to gas at the destination.|A cargo's margin is the sale price less feed gas, liquefaction, shipping and regas.|Feed gas demand is terminal capacity times utilisation plus plant fuel.
4.9|034|TTF is the European gas benchmark; JKM is the Northeast Asian LNG benchmark.|The export arbitrage is open when the netback to the US Gulf exceeds Henry Hub-based cost.|Shipping and tolling are the wedges between destination price and US price.|Regional prices connect when cargoes can move and decouple when capacity is full.|Spread scenarios show which destination pulls cargoes and when lifting stops.
4.10|036|An outage scenario states which asset fails, by how much and for how long.|Gas reroutes over alternative paths until they fill.|Storage near the market covers the gap up to its deliverability.|What cannot be rerouted shows up as price spikes and wider basis.|A winter stress case combines high demand with lost supply on the same days.
6.1|045|Some states kept regulated utilities; others restructured to competitive wholesale and retail markets.|Wholesale is generators selling to utilities and retailers; retail is sales to end users.|Wholesale sales and transmission are federal; retail rates and distribution are state matters.|Markets sell energy, capacity, ancillary services and transmission rights.|About two thirds of US load sits inside ISO/RTO markets; the Southeast and much of the West trade bilaterally.
6.2|046|An ISO dispatches generation, runs markets, manages transmission and settles participants.|Operations follow a daily timeline: bids, day-ahead clearing, reliability commitment, real-time dispatch, settlement.|Reliability coordination keeps flows within limits and manages emergencies.|ISOs plan regional transmission expansion.|Rules change through stakeholder committees and regulator approval.
6.3|045|PJM and MISO are large multi-state markets with nodal energy pricing.|SPP covers the central plains; ERCOT is an energy-only Texas market under state oversight.|CAISO runs California and a wider Western real-time imbalance market.|NYISO and ISO-NE are single-state and six-state markets with tight gas dependence.|Markets differ in capacity mechanisms, scarcity pricing and settlement detail.
6.4|046|Outside ISOs, utilities self-schedule and trade bilaterally.|Integrated resource plans decide what those utilities build or contract.|Bilateral power trades at named hubs.|Transmission service is reserved through each provider's OASIS site.|Western day-ahead market initiatives are extending organized trading into bilateral regions.
6.5|047|The day-ahead market clears hourly schedules and prices for tomorrow.|The real-time market redispatches every few minutes to follow actual conditions.|Day-ahead quantities settle at day-ahead prices; deviations settle at real-time prices.|Virtual bids take financial positions between the two markets and help prices converge.|The DA-RT spread shows forecast error and risk premium.
6.6|048|The price is set by the cost of the next unit needed to serve one more MW.|With congestion, that marginal cost differs by location: locational marginal price.|Nodes are individual buses; zones and hubs are averages used for load and trading.|Offer caps limit prices; scarcity pricing raises them when reserves run short.|An LMP can be computed from a dispatch model as the dual of each node's balance.
6.7|048|The energy component is the system-wide marginal cost at the reference bus.|The congestion component is the cost of binding transmission limits at that node.|The loss component reflects marginal transmission losses.|Each binding constraint has a shadow price: the saving from one more MW of limit.|LMP equals energy plus congestion plus losses.
6.8|056|Lines are limited by heating, voltage and stability.|Operators keep the system secure against the loss of any single element (N-1).|A shift factor says how much of an injection at a node flows on a given line.|The same constraints bind repeatedly, creating predictable congestion patterns.|Constraint analysis shows who gains and who pays when a limit binds.
6.9|051|Capacity markets pay resources to be available in future peak periods.|Accreditation discounts nameplate to expected availability at peak; ELCC does this for variable resources.|A sloped demand curve sets the price where offers meet it.|Load-serving entities must hold capacity equal to peak load plus a reserve margin.|Capacity revenue is accredited MW times the clearing price times the period.
6.10|050|Regulation follows second-to-second imbalances.|Operating reserves stand ready to replace a lost unit within minutes.|Energy and reserves are cleared together so each unit provides its most valuable mix.|A settlement statement lists every charge and credit by interval.|Reconciling charge codes against your own shadow calculation finds billing errors.
7.1|053|Thermal plants burn gas, coal or oil; combined cycles are efficient, peakers are flexible.|Nuclear runs flat out; hydro is flexible but limited by water.|Wind and solar have near-zero marginal cost and weather-driven output.|Ramp rates, minimum run times and start costs limit how units follow prices.|Compare technologies on cost, flexibility and availability, not capacity alone.
7.2|054|Heat rate is fuel input per unit of electricity, in MMBtu per MWh; lower is better.|Incremental heat rate is the fuel for the next MWh; average includes the no-load fuel.|Variable O&M is the non-fuel cost that scales with output.|Marginal cost is incremental heat rate times fuel price plus variable O&M.|A heat rate curve shows efficiency improving toward full load.
7.3|061|A combined cycle has higher capital cost and lower heat rate than a peaker, so it runs more hours.|Each start costs fuel and wear; cycling raises maintenance.|Capacity factor depends on where the unit sits in the merit order.|Fuel can be firm, interruptible or bought daily.|Unit margin is power revenue less fuel, variable O&M and start costs.
7.4|055|Spark spread is power price minus heat rate times gas price.|The clean spark spread also subtracts emissions cost.|Implied heat rate is power price divided by gas price.|Gas must be bought on the gas-day schedule before power needs are certain.|A generator hedges by selling power and buying gas in heat-rate proportion.
7.5|049|Merit order stacks units from cheapest to dearest; demand sets the marginal unit.|Unit commitment decides which units to start, given start costs and limits.|Minimum up and down times stop units switching on and off freely.|Security-constrained dispatch honours transmission limits.|Dispatch cost is the sum of each unit's output times its marginal cost.
7.6|062|Wind and solar forecasts combine weather models with plant data.|Error is measured with MAE, RMSE and bias.|A forecast miss is bought or sold at the real-time price.|Net load is demand minus wind and solar.|Evaluate forecasts against a naive benchmark.
7.7|062|Curtailment happens when output cannot be delivered or is not wanted.|Prices go negative when generators pay to keep running.|Economic curtailment follows price; reliability curtailment is an operator order.|Contract terms decide who bears curtailed energy.|Curtailment value is lost energy times the price plus lost credits.
7.8|063|A battery is defined by power (MW), energy (MWh), efficiency and state-of-charge limits.|State of charge rises with charging and falls with discharging.|Round-trip efficiency is the share of charged energy returned.|Arbitrage charges in cheap hours and discharges in dear ones.|Batteries stack energy arbitrage with ancillary service revenue.
7.9|064|Long-duration storage includes pumped hydro and other multi-hour to multi-day technologies.|Seasonal balancing needs far more energy than daily shifting.|A dunkelflaute is a multi-day stretch of low wind and solar.|Each added hour of duration earns less than the one before.|Storage counts toward adequacy only for as long as it can discharge.
7.10|065|Portfolio dispatch runs several assets against one price view.|Co-optimising beats optimising each asset alone.|Hedges are layered on the expected physical position.|Shared limits such as an interconnection cap couple the assets.|The objective is total margin subject to every constraint.
