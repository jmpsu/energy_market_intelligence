1.1|001|Upstream finds and produces, midstream gathers, processes, moves and stores, downstream delivers to end users.|Gas and NGLs can be stored and shipped as molecules; electricity must be produced the instant it is used.|Pipelines, wires and utility distribution earn regulated rates; production, generation and trading mostly earn market prices.|FERC regulates interstate transmission and wholesale sales; states and provinces regulate retail, siting and intrastate systems.|A structure map shows who hands the commodity to whom; each handoff is a contract, a meter and a data feed.
1.2|002|Producers sell commodity-price exposure; processors turn raw gas into residue gas and NGLs for a fee or a share.|Pipelines and storage sell capacity under tariffs; LDCs buy gas and deliver it to homes and businesses at regulated rates.|Generators sell energy, capacity and ancillary services; load-serving entities buy them to meet customer demand.|Marketers and traders connect buyers and sellers and warehouse risk; financial counterparties provide hedges without touching the molecule.|One delivered molecule sits under a chain of contracts: purchase, gathering, processing, transport, storage, sale.
1.3|024|A physical contract obliges delivery of a quantity at a point and time; failure triggers cover costs or penalties.|A financial contract settles in cash against a reference price; no molecule or electron moves.|Hedgers reduce an existing exposure, speculators take one on, asset-backed traders optimise physical assets around the market.|At delivery the financial contract and the physical price at the same point must meet, or arbitrage closes the gap.|Use physical when you need the commodity at a point; use financial when you only need the price outcome.
1.4|004|Gas stores in reservoirs and linepack; power has almost no storage, so supply and demand balance every second.|Gas moves at pipeline speed over hours to days; power flows near instantly along every parallel path by physics.|Gas trades by the gas day; power settles hourly or every five minutes, so power data is far more granular.|Limited storage and steep supply curves make power prices spike harder and revert faster than gas prices.|Gas plants often set the power price, and power demand drives gas burn, so each market moves the other.
1.5|045|An interconnection is a set of grids running in synchronism at one frequency; trouble anywhere is felt everywhere inside it.|The Eastern Interconnection is the largest in load and capacity and contains several ISOs plus bilateral regions.|The Western Interconnection is long-distance, hydro and solar rich, and coordinated under WECC.|ERCOT is its own interconnection with only small DC ties, so it can import little during scarcity.|Physical transfer limits cap flows; commercial access also needs transmission service or market membership.
1.6|045|An ISO or RTO runs the grid and the wholesale market for a region without owning generation or wires.|Vertically integrated utilities own generation, transmission and distribution and recover costs through regulated rates.|Independent power producers own merchant plants and live on market revenue or contracts.|Power marketers buy and resell energy and manage portfolios without necessarily owning assets.|Balancing authorities keep supply and demand matched in their area; reliability coordinators watch the wider system.
1.7|046|A bilateral deal is negotiated between two parties and needs transmission arranged separately in non-ISO regions.|Organized markets clear all bids and offers centrally and settle against published prices.|OTC trades are customised and carry counterparty credit risk; exchange trades are standard and cleared.|Clearing replaces counterparty risk with margin: initial margin up front, variation margin daily.|Price discovery is strongest where trading is concentrated: liquid hubs, exchanges and ISO markets.
1.8|024|Gas is priced per MMBtu, measured in Mcf or Bcf; power in MWh; NGLs in gallons or barrels. Conversions are the first check on any number.|A future is exchange-traded and margined; a forward is a bilateral contract for later delivery.|A swap exchanges a fixed price for a floating index; a basis instrument prices a location relative to a benchmark.|Options give the right, not the obligation, to buy or sell; structured products bundle several of these.|Capacity, renewable certificates and emissions allowances are separate products with their own prices.
1.9|007|Price reporting agencies survey trades and publish indices that contracts settle against.|ISOs publish prices, load, generation and constraints, usually free and in bulk.|EIA and regulators publish production, storage, flows and filings; coverage is broad, timing is lagged.|Pipeline bulletin boards post capacity, scheduled volumes and notices each cycle.|Licences limit redistribution and derived use; a product must track where every data point came from.
1.10|009|Value is added or lost at each step from wellhead to burner tip: gathering, processing, transport, distribution.|A gas plant converts MMBtu to MWh at its heat rate, which ties the gas price to the power price.|Each stage captures a margin: fees for infrastructure, spreads for traders, spark spread for generators.|Modeling gas and power together captures feedback that separate models miss.|A reference model fixes locations, units and identifiers so every later analysis joins cleanly.
2.1|010|Major US gas comes from a few shale basins; each has different costs, liquids content and distance to market.|Growth rate matters as much as level: fast-growing basins outrun their pipelines.|Shale wells decline steeply, so steady output needs continuous drilling.|Netback is the market price minus the cost to get there; breakeven is the price at which a new well earns its return.|A basin with too little takeaway capacity sells at a discount until new pipe arrives.
2.2|013|Gathering lines collect gas from many wells at low pressure and deliver it to processing.|Compression raises pressure for transport; treating removes CO2, H2S and water.|A plant takes wet inlet gas and outputs pipeline-quality residue gas plus an NGL stream.|Removing NGLs and burning plant fuel shrinks the gas volume and energy that reaches the tailgate.|Fee contracts pay per unit, POP shares proceeds, keep-whole returns the producer's Btus and leaves the processor exposed to the frac spread.
2.3|013|Raw gas is mostly methane with ethane, propane, butanes and heavier components plus impurities.|Gas is sold on energy (MMBtu), so heat content converts volume to value.|Associated gas is a by-product of oil wells; it flows whatever the gas price while oil economics work.|Pipelines set limits on heat content, dew point and contaminants; off-spec gas can be refused.|Wet gas earns extra NGL revenue but needs processing; dry gas does not.
2.4|026|Supply is production plus imports plus storage withdrawals.|Demand is residential/commercial, industrial, power burn, LNG and pipeline exports.|Heating and cooling degree days translate temperature into demand.|The weekly storage change is the market's scorecard of the balance.|A balance table that does not sum to zero has a data error or a missing flow.
2.5|018|Interstate pipelines are FERC-regulated open-access carriers that sell transport, not gas.|Intrastate systems, large in Texas, operate under state rules.|A few long-haul corridors move gas from producing basins to demand centres and export points.|Shippers put gas in at receipt points and take it out at delivery points named in their contract.|Interconnects and market centres let gas change pipelines and are where hubs form.
2.6|014|Nameplate is design capacity; operational capacity is what the pipeline can move today.|Contracted capacity is sold under firm agreements; available capacity is what remains to sell.|Utilisation is scheduled volume divided by operational capacity.|A constraint point is a segment or compressor that fills before the rest of the system.|When a constraint binds, prices upstream fall and prices downstream rise.
2.7|025|Depleted reservoirs hold the most gas; salt caverns cycle fastest; aquifers sit between.|Working gas can be withdrawn; base gas stays to hold pressure.|Ratchets cut allowed injection or withdrawal rates as inventory moves.|Storage fills in the low-demand months and empties in winter.|Deliverability is the daily rate, not the inventory: plenty of gas in the ground can still be too slow on a peak day.
2.8|028|The gas day is a 24-hour delivery period with several nomination cycles.|A nomination requests a flow; both ends must confirm it; the pipeline then schedules it.|When requests exceed capacity, the pipeline cuts lowest-priority service first.|Measured flows are allocated to shippers after the day and rarely match schedules exactly.|A scheduler nominates, checks confirmations, fixes cuts and reconciles actuals every day.
2.9|029|An imbalance is the gap between what a shipper scheduled and what actually flowed.|Linepack, the gas held in the pipe, absorbs small short-term mismatches.|An operational flow order tightens tolerances when the system is stressed.|Critical notices and force majeure declarations warn of reduced service.|Imbalances beyond tolerance are cashed out at penal prices.
2.10|012|Delivered cost is purchase price plus transport charges plus fuel.|Fuel retention is gas kept by the pipeline to run compressors; commodity charges are per unit shipped.|Reservation charges are paid for capacity whether used or not; usage charges only when gas flows.|Realised price at the delivery point is the market price there less everything paid to arrive.|Transport is worth holding when the basis spread exceeds its variable cost.
3.1|019|Henry Hub in Louisiana is the delivery point of the main US gas futures contract and the pricing reference for most deals.|The contract is a standard monthly volume in MMBtu for physical delivery at the hub.|Trading in a month ends shortly before delivery; the final settlement price is widely used in indices.|The prompt month is the nearest contract; published prices come from exchanges and price reporting agencies.|Henry Hub does not tell you the price in a constrained region; that is what basis measures.
3.2|019|Basis is the local price minus Henry Hub; negative means the location is cheaper.|Supply areas with excess gas trade at a discount, market areas with tight delivery at a premium.|A hub with no spare takeaway can trade below zero: sellers pay to dispose of gas.|Basis is seasonal and can be more volatile than the benchmark itself.|Basis P&L is position times the change in basis, independent of the Henry Hub move.
3.3|024|Day-ahead cash gas trades for next-day flow at each location.|Bidweek deals at the end of a month set the monthly index for the next one.|Daily indices average each day's cash trades; monthly indices come from bidweek.|An index deal floats with the published price; a fixed-price deal locks it.|A book's index exposure is the volume that will price at a not-yet-known index.
3.4|024|Futures are marked daily; losses must be covered with variation margin in cash.|A fixed-for-floating swap pays the difference between a fixed price and the index.|A call caps a buyer's price, a put floors a seller's, a collar does both at lower cost.|A physical forward specifies point, volume, term and delivery obligations.|Choose the instrument by what must be fixed: price, basis, volume or delivery.
3.5|023|A forward curve is built from quoted contract prices by delivery month.|Contango means later months cost more; backwardation the reverse; gas adds a winter premium.|A calendar spread is the price difference between two delivery months.|A strip is the average over consecutive months, such as a winter or summer strip.|Curve shape tells you what the market pays to store, and where it expects tightness.
3.6|016|Firm transport lets you buy at one point and sell at another, so its value moves like an option on the spread.|A basis swap fixes the difference between a location and Henry Hub.|Hedging a regional position needs both a benchmark hedge and a basis hedge.|Transport value is the spread less variable cost, compared with the reservation charge.|A hedge is effective when its P&L offsets the exposure; a Henry Hub hedge alone leaves basis open.
3.7|025|Intrinsic value is the spread you can lock today by buying a cheap month and selling a dear one.|Extrinsic value is the extra from re-optimising as spreads move.|Injection and withdrawal fees plus fuel reduce the captured spread.|Storage monetises the summer-winter spread.|Capacity and ratchet limits cap how much of the spread can be captured.
3.8|033|LNG feed gas demand depends on terminal capacity, utilisation and global spreads.|A tolling customer pays a fixed liquefaction fee whether or not it lifts cargoes.|Netback is the destination price less regasification, shipping and liquefaction costs.|Many US contracts price LNG as a Henry Hub multiple plus a fixed fee.|High global prices pull US gas into export and tighten the domestic balance up to terminal capacity.
3.9|027|Positions are summed by hub and delivery month to see net exposure.|Mark-to-market values each position at the current curve.|A regional position splits into a Henry Hub exposure and a basis exposure.|Daily P&L is position times price change plus the effect of new trades.|Limits cap position size or risk by trader, book and location.
3.10|027|Basis risk is the chance that the local price moves differently from the hedge.|Volume risk comes from demand or production differing from plan, often in the worst price conditions.|Operational risk covers outages, cuts and human error.|Price risk grows when a market is illiquid and positions cannot be closed at the quoted price.|A risk report shows positions, P&L, limits and the biggest drivers on one page.
